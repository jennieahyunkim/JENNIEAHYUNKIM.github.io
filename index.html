01 · Customer analytics · Industry internship

Spotting a demand shift before the sourcing decision

I used point-of-sale data to show MABROS Jewellery where customer demand was moving, in time for the team to act on it.

RoleCustomer Analytics & Sourcing Intern
WhereMABROS Jewellery, Hong Kong
WhenSummer 2026
ToolsR, SQL
12%

shift in customer demand toward gemstone designs

9%

fewer stockouts after automated reorder models

100+

SKUs analysed across 3 retail locations

Context

MABROS is a jewellery retailer in Hong Kong with three retail locations and more than 100 SKUs (individual products). In jewellery, sourcing commits budget to designs before anyone knows how they will sell. The team needed evidence of what customers were choosing.

My role

I owned the sales data analysis and the weekly tracking of KPIs (key performance indicators). The operations team built the reorder models, and I supplied the numbers they ran on.

Goal

Give the sales team clear purchase insights ahead of sourcing decisions, and help operations keep the right pieces in stock.

Audience

The customers I studied were MABROS retail clients across the three stores. The people using my work were the sales team, who advise clients and guide sourcing, and the operations team, who manage stock.

Strategy

Start from transactions, not opinions. I segmented sales to see where demand was moving, then tracked sell-through (the share of stock sold) and stock turnover every week so the team could act on the shift in reorders, not only read about it.

Execution
  • I segmented POS data across 100+ SKUs in R to compare demand by design type.
  • I quantified the shift toward gemstone designs and delivered the purchase insights to the sales team ahead of sourcing decisions.
  • I tracked weekly sell-through and stock turnover across the three locations in SQL.
  • I supported the operations team as they built automated reorder models on those KPIs.
Results

The analysis showed a 12% shift in customer demand toward gemstone designs. The reorder models cut stockouts by 9%.

The sales data is confidential to MABROS, so I describe the method here and do not show the files.

Learnings

What I would repeat: timing the analysis to the decision. The finding mattered because the sales team had it before sourcing was committed.

What I would change: I would compare a longer period, so a seasonal bump is not mistaken for a lasting change in taste.

Trade-off: I kept the comparison simple, by design type, so the sales team could use it with clients straight away. A finer breakdown would have been more precise but slower to act on.

02 · Segmentation modeling · Academic project

Who are H&M's most valuable customers?

I grouped 1.17 million H&M customers by how recently, how often and how much they buy. One customer in ten turned out to account for half of all purchase value.

RoleBuilt the team's analysis notebook: data cleaning and segmentation
WhereUSC Marshall, team of seven
WhenFall 2026
ToolsR (tidyverse, k-means)
49.8%

of purchase value comes from the top 10.3% of customers

4

customer segments found with k-means

31M+

transactions in the public H&M dataset

Context

H&M is a global fashion retailer selling in stores and online. Its profit was growing while sales stayed flat, so the question was where marketing money would do the most. Our team used H&M's public transaction dataset to ask which customers to invest in, with which products, and through which channel.

My role

I built the shared R Markdown notebook the team worked from. I owned the data preparation and ran the customer segmentation with one teammate. Other teammates then compared the segments and ran the regression checks in the same notebook.

Goal

Answer two questions. Who are H&M's most valuable customer segments, and what share of revenue do they bring? How do those segments differ in age, product categories, average price paid and channel (in-store or online)?

Audience

The analysis is written for a marketing or CRM (customer relationship management) team deciding which customers to prioritise.

Strategy

I used RFM scoring, which rates each customer on Recency (how recently they bought), Frequency (how often) and Monetary value (how much they spent). I then used k-means clustering, a method that groups similar customers together automatically, so the segments come from behaviour and not from guesses about demographics.

Execution
  • I loaded the transaction, customer and product tables in R and limited them to 20 September 2018 to 29 February 2020, before store closures distorted the channel mix.
  • I recovered purchase quantity from the raw rows and checked that units and spend still matched the source: 22.9M purchase rows from 1.17M customers.
  • I calculated recency, frequency and spend for every customer, then log-transformed and standardized them so no single measure dominated.
  • I chose four segments with the elbow method on a sample of 100,000 customers, then assigned all 1.17M customers.
  • I repeated the clustering with a new sample and seed. The same four segments came back, with sizes differing by fewer than 20 customers.
Results

Four segments came out of the clustering. The Loyal top spenders are 10.3% of customers and account for 49.8% of observed purchase value. Together with the Regulars, about 38% of customers drive 83% of value.

  • Loyal top spenders shopped on 21.4 days on average and last bought 39 days before the end of the period. They bought 79% of their units online.
  • Regulars are 28.0% of customers and 33.5% of value. They bought 42% of their units in stores, the highest store share of any segment.
  • The other two segments are 62% of customers and 17% of value.

The team's recommendation was to retain the Loyal top spenders, test growth among the Regulars, and validate both with an experiment before spending more.

View the full R analysis

Prices in the dataset are scaled, so "value" means relative purchase value and not revenue in a real currency.

Open the final deck (PDF)

The team's final deck. The segment results are on slides 6 and 7, and the channel comparison is on slide 8.

Learnings

What I would repeat: building one shared notebook with checks at each step, so seven people worked from the same customer-level data and every number could be traced.

Trade-off: k-means needs the number of segments chosen in advance. The elbow chart flattened after four, and four groups are few enough for a marketing team to name and target.

Limit to keep in mind: the segments are built from spending and frequency, so part of the value gap is built in. The data is also historical, and it describes customers without proving how they would respond to a campaign. That is why the recommendation ends with an experiment.

03 · Product strategy and prototyping · Case competition

From strategy slide to clickable product

I set the target market and product strategy for FLUO's AI wallet, then built a working front-end demo so the judges could click through the idea.

RoleTarget market and product strategy lead, prototype builder
WhereFLUO AI Wallet Case Competition, Los Angeles
WhenOctober 2026
ToolsClaude Code
1

full front-end prototype of the AI agent and Smart Wallet

3

connected features: Dining Rewards, AI Money Buddy and Smart Wallet

Context

FLUO is a money app for college students, building an AI agent and a Smart Wallet. The competition asked teams to recommend how FLUO should take the product to market, and to pitch it to FLUO's founder and a panel of fintech executives.

My role

On a five-person team, I developed the target-market and product strategy. I also built the prototype on my own.

Goal

Convince the panel of two things: that we had picked the right first customers, and that the product we described could actually be built.

Audience

College students. I recommended that FLUO launch with domestic students who live off campus without a dining plan, usually sophomores and above, and expand to international students second.

Domestic students came first because their problem is one FLUO can solve at launch. They pay for every meal, so a dining reward gives them a reason to try the app. International students need help with a new financial system and U.S. credit, which depends on partnerships that come later.

Strategy

Pick one segment to win first and sequence the rest. I positioned FLUO around food budgeting, the main money problem we identified for these students, and shaped the product as one bundle of three connected features: Dining Rewards to save, an AI Money Buddy to personalise advice, and a Smart Wallet to see spending, savings and rewards in one view.

Then show the product instead of describing it: a clickable demo makes a strategy easier to believe than a slide does.

Execution
  • I developed the target-market and product strategy sections of the pitch.
  • I built a full front-end prototype of the AI agent and Smart Wallet using Claude Code.
  • I turned the team's strategy into a clickable demo for the final pitch.
  • I pitched with the team to FLUO's founder and fintech executives.
Results

We presented a working demo alongside the deck in the final pitch to FLUO's founder and a panel of fintech executives. The demo covers all three features and runs on a phone.

Open the live demo View the pitch deck (PDF)

The demo uses sample balances and prepared replies. No bank account is connected.

Learnings

What building it taught me: a prototype forces decisions a slide can skip. For each screen I had to decide what a student sees first and what the app does next.

Trade-off: the demo uses sample data and prepared replies, not a live AI or a bank connection. That kept it reliable in a live pitch, at the cost of realism.

What I would do next: our student and restaurant interviews were planned, not completed. The first step is to run them and test which of the three features students value most.

04 · Launch marketing and partnerships · Industry internship

Marketing a bond launch that drew S$1B in demand

I made the sales materials and helped secure the advertising partnerships behind a private equity bond launch on Singapore's stock exchange.

RolePrivate Equity Investment Solutions & Marketing Intern
WhereAzalea Asset Management, Singapore
WhenSummer 2024
ToolsPowerPoint, Canva
S$1B

in demand for the bond launch I supported

2

advertising channels negotiated: radio and digital

Context

Azalea Asset Management is a subsidiary of Temasek Holdings that creates private equity investment products. It was launching a private equity bond on Singapore's stock exchange. Private equity is hard to explain quickly, so the launch depended on clear materials and wide reach.

My role

I designed the distributor-facing materials and negotiated advertising partnership terms, working with the internal legal team. The launch itself was a full-team effort and the S$1B figure belongs to that team.

Goal

Give distributors what they needed to sell the bond, and maximize the reach of the launch.

Audience

Two groups: the distributors who sell the bond, and the investors reached through radio and digital advertising.

Strategy

Work on two tracks at once. Equip the people selling the product with simple, visual explanations, and reach investors directly through channels they already use every day.

Execution
  • I designed pitch decks and product highlight sheets for distributors in PowerPoint and Canva.
  • I negotiated deal terms for advertising partnerships on radio (CLASS 95) and digital platforms (Grab).
  • I collaborated with the legal team on those terms.
Results

The bond launch drew S$1B in demand.

Public record: news release on the public offer result, filed with the Singapore Exchange in July 2024.

The distributor materials are internal to Azalea, so I describe them here and do not show them.

Learnings

What I learned: in a regulated product every claim has to be exact. Working with the legal team taught me to simplify how something is explained without changing what it says.

What I would change: I would agree with each advertising partner at the start on how reach would be reported, so the channels could be compared after the launch.

Trade-off: radio reaches a broad audience, and a digital platform reaches people in an app they use every day. Using both split the effort but covered more of the audience.

Daily dashboards and a cleaner product catalog

I built the daily reporting the marketing team used to move ad spend, and rebuilt the store's Shopify catalog so shoppers could filter it.

RoleeCommerce Operations Intern
WhereMementomori, Seoul
WhenMay to December 2023
ToolsExcel, Shopify
150+

SKUs rebuilt in the Shopify catalog

10+

customer KPIs tracked every day

Context

Mementomori is an online brand based in Seoul. The marketing team set ad budgets monthly and needed a current view of which categories were performing. The product catalog also used inconsistent tags and descriptions, which made it hard to browse.

My role

I built and maintained the dashboards and rebuilt the catalog. The marketing team made the budget decisions.

Goal

Help marketing put monthly ad spend behind the categories that perform, and make products easier for shoppers to find.

Audience

The marketing team used the dashboards. Online shoppers used the catalog.

Strategy

Fix the information first. Reliable daily numbers let the team spend with confidence, and consistent product data lets shoppers filter to what they want.

Execution
  • I built and updated daily Excel dashboards tracking 10+ customer KPIs, including AOV (average order value) and churn (customers who stop buying).
  • I rebuilt the Shopify catalog for 150+ SKUs by standardizing inventory tags and descriptions.
  • I set up filterable listings to improve the browsing experience.
Results

The marketing team used the dashboards to reallocate monthly ad spend toward high-performing categories. All 150+ products became filterable.

The dashboards and store data are internal to Mementomori, so I describe the work here and do not show it.

Learnings

What I would repeat: standardising the product data before anything else. Clean tags made both the filters and the reporting more reliable.

What I would change: I updated the dashboards by hand each day. Today I would automate the data pull, so the time goes to reading the numbers.

Trade-off: the marketing team already worked in Excel, so I built there and not in a more powerful tool they would have had to learn.
